How race time prediction works

This predictor uses the Riegel formula, published by engineer Pete Riegel in 1981 and still the most widely used race prediction model: T2 = T1 × (D2 ÷ D1)1.06. The exponent 1.06 captures how much runners slow down as the distance grows — roughly 6% more time per doubling of distance beyond simple proportionality. It has held up remarkably well across four decades of race data for distances from 1500 m to the marathon.

Formula and sources

The page implements that equation and no other adjustment for age, sex, or mileage. Distances are the standard road lengths: 5 km, 10 km, 21.0975 km, and 42.195 km. The source is Pete Riegel, "Athletic Records and Human Endurance," American Scientist 69 (1981). The same exponent is used for every distance pair, including a 5K-to-marathon jump — the formula does not steepen itself when the gap gets large.

A worked example

A 25:00 5K is 1,500 seconds. The function on this page returns:

  • 10K — 52:07 (5:13/km), not 50:00. Doubling the distance does not double the time.
  • Half marathon — 1:55:00 (5:27/km).
  • Marathon — 3:59:47 (5:41/km).

The marathon figure is what the exponent produces from a 5K. It is not a promise that a 25:00 5K runner is ready to run 3:59. The guide on the limits of the Riegel formula is the longer version of that warning.

Limitations

  • Adjacent distances (5K to 10K, 10K to half) are where the exponent was meant to be used. A 5K does not measure marathon fueling or long-run durability, so the marathon line is a ceiling for a fair day, not a race plan.
  • The input has to be a recent all-out effort. A stale personal best or a workout where you held back is fed into the same equation and looks just as precise.
  • Heat, hills, and wind are not in the formula. Neither is a different exponent. Some coaches use something steeper than 1.06 for the half-to-marathon jump; this page does not.

How to get an accurate prediction

  • Use a recent, all-out result. A race or honest time trial from the last 6–8 weeks reflects your current fitness. A PR from two years ago does not.
  • Predict adjacent distances. A 5K predicts a 10K very well, a half marathon reasonably well, and a marathon only if you've done the training volume. Marathon predictions assume marathon-appropriate endurance.
  • Adjust for conditions. Heat, hills, and wind all slow real races. Treat the prediction as your ceiling on a fair day, not a guarantee.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the Riegel formula?

For well-trained runners predicting adjacent distances (5K→10K, 10K→half), it's usually within 1–2%. Marathon predictions are less reliable because they depend heavily on endurance training — many runners run 10–20 minutes slower than their 5K-predicted marathon time if their weekly mileage is low.

Why is my predicted marathon time so fast?

The formula assumes your endurance scales with your speed. If you haven't built marathon-specific mileage (long runs of 28 km+ and consistent 50 km+ weeks), expect to be slower than predicted. Use the prediction as a training target, not a race-day pace plan.

What race result should I use as input?

Your most recent all-out effort at a shorter distance — ideally an official race, or a solo time trial where you genuinely emptied the tank. The fresher and more honest the input, the better the prediction.

Can I use this to plan my race pace?

Yes — divide the predicted time by the distance to get target pace (shown in the table). Many runners aim for the predicted pace through 80% of the race and speed up at the end if they feel strong.

Is there a better model than Riegel?

VDOT (Jack Daniels) and Cameron's model give similar results for most runners. Riegel is the simplest and most transparent. For training zones rather than race predictions, use our VDOT-based Training Zone Calculator.

Common mistakes

  • Predicting too far up the distance ladder. A 5K predicts a 10K reliably; the same 5K predicting a marathon is a much bigger stretch, since marathon performance depends on endurance a short race never tests. Read more in our guide on the limits of race prediction formulas.
  • Treating the prediction as a guarantee. It's a fair-conditions ceiling, not a promise — heat, hills, and an underdone taper will all eat into it on race day.
  • Ignoring how long ago the reference race was. Fitness shifts over weeks and months; a result from last season won't accurately predict this season's race.
