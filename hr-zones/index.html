How heart rate zones are calculated

This calculator uses the Karvonen method, which bases zones on your heart rate reserve — the gap between resting and maximum heart rate — rather than a simple percentage of max. The formula is: target HR = resting HR + (max HR − resting HR) × intensity%. Because it accounts for your resting heart rate, it personalizes zones better than %max alone: two runners with the same max HR but different fitness levels get different (and more accurate) zones.

If you leave resting HR blank, the calculator falls back to a straight percentage of max HR — less precise but still useful. If you don't know your max HR, enter your age and the calculator estimates it with the Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age), which research shows is more accurate than the traditional "220 minus age".

Formula and sources

  • Karvonen (Karvonen, Kentala, and Mustala, 1957): target HR = resting HR + (max HR − resting HR) × intensity. Leave resting heart rate blank and the same formula reduces to a percentage of max, because there is no reserve to apply.
  • Tanaka, Monahan, and Seals, "Age-predicted maximal heart rate revisited," Journal of the American College of Cardiology 37 (2001): max HR = 208 − 0.7 × age, rounded to the nearest beat. Age 40 is 180. "220 − age" is not used.
  • Intensities on this page — easy 50–60%, Zone 2 60–70%, tempo 75–85%, threshold 83–90%, VO2max 90–100% of heart-rate reserve. Those cut points match the training-zone language on the Training Zone Calculator. They are not a universal five-zone standard, and a watch that slices max heart rate into equal fifths will disagree.

A worked example

Age 40 and a resting heart rate of 60 bpm. Tanaka gives a max of 180. Heart-rate reserve is 120. The zones this page returns:

  • Easy — 120–132 bpm
  • Zone 2 — 132–144 bpm
  • Tempo — 150–162 bpm
  • Threshold — 160–168 bpm
  • VO2max — 168–180 bpm

Tempo and threshold overlap because the intensity bands overlap (75–85% and 83–90%). That is the table, not a rounding error. The guide on estimating max heart rate explains why an age formula is only a starting point, and the guide on Karvonen versus percent of max shows what changes when resting heart rate is left out.

Limitations

  • An age-based max is a population average. Individual max heart rates at the same age commonly differ by enough to move a zone boundary.
  • Resting heart rate moves with sleep, caffeine, and illness. One morning reading is usable; a several-day average is stabler.
  • Wrist optical sensors lag during hard efforts and often miss a true peak. A chest strap at the end of a race is a better max than the formula.
  • These zones are not a medical clearance to do a max-heart-rate test.

What each zone is for

  • Jog / Easy (50–60%) — recovery runs and warm-ups. Builds the aerobic base with minimal stress.
  • Zone 2 (60–70%) — the foundation of endurance training. Most weekly volume belongs here; you should be able to hold a conversation.
  • Tempo (75–85%) — "comfortably hard" sustained efforts that improve your lactate clearance.
  • Threshold (83–90%) — roughly one-hour race effort; cruise intervals and threshold runs live here.
  • VO2max (90–100%) — short hard intervals (3–5 minutes) that raise your aerobic ceiling.

Common mistakes

  • Using "220 minus age" for max HR. This decades-old shortcut has a wide error margin. The Tanaka formula this calculator uses (208 − 0.7 × age) fits modern research better, and a real field test beats either estimate.
  • Skipping resting HR. Without it, zones fall back to a flat percentage of max, which ignores your actual fitness. Two runners with the same max HR but very different aerobic bases end up with meaningfully different real zones.
  • Chasing a pace instead of the zone. On a hot day or hilly route, hitting your usual easy pace can push heart rate into tempo territory. See our guide on pace vs. heart rate for when to let go of the pace target.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find my real max heart rate?

Age formulas are estimates with a standard deviation of about ±10 bpm. For a real number, look at the highest value your HR monitor has recorded at the end of a hard race or all-out hill repeats. A field test: after a thorough warm-up, run 3 × 2 minutes uphill at maximal effort — the peak reading is close to your true max.

How do I measure resting heart rate?

Check your heart rate first thing in the morning before getting out of bed, for several days, and take the average. Most watches with sleep tracking report it automatically.

Why is Zone 2 training so popular?

Zone 2 builds mitochondrial density and fat-burning capacity with low injury risk, so you can accumulate a lot of it. Most successful endurance programs put roughly 80% of training time at or below Zone 2 intensity.

Karvonen vs. percentage of max — which should I use?

Karvonen (enter both max and resting HR) is more personalized because it accounts for fitness via resting heart rate. Use %max only if you don't know your resting HR. The zones from the two methods can differ by 5–15 bpm at the low end.

My watch shows different zones. Which is right?

Watch vendors use different zone models (some use 5 zones of %max, some use lactate-threshold anchored zones). None is "wrong" — what matters is using one model consistently. This calculator's zones map to common running training language (easy/tempo/threshold/VO2max).
